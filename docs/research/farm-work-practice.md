# How the work was done: Aroostook potato farms, about 1941 to 1984

A sourced reference for the work the game animates (gathered 14 November 2026 by a research pass over period bulletins, catalogues and field reports). Tags are explained below; figures marked [EST] have no source.

Most of the numbers come from Maine Agricultural Experiment Station (MAES) bulletins, USDA bulletins, period machinery catalogues, and one 1970 report written by observers who picked alongside Aroostook crews for several days. The gaps are mostly the 1960s–80s: the Maine bulletins for those decades are cited elsewhere but I could not get them, because UMaine DigitalCommons closed on 30 Jun 2026 and HathiTrust and Maine Memory Network blocked automated access.

Tags: **[ME]** Maine source, **[US]** national or elsewhere (place named), **[MOD]** modern, **[DERIVED]** my arithmetic on sourced numbers, **[EST]** no source found, with what it rests on.

## 1. Spring tillage
- Aroostook norm was **fall plowing**: "snow does not leave the land early enough to permit spring plowing … fall plowing is customary". USDA FB 1810, 1938, p.67. [ME]
- The 1964 Aroostook sequence: fall plow, **2 spring harrowings**, plant, pre-emergence chemical, 2 cultivations, 1 spading, so 9 trips over the field. SoilCons64 [ME]
- The harrow was **spring-tooth**: "Disk harrows were not used very much." Fields were harrowed **2.9 times**, at **0.6 h/acre per pass**. Schrumpf41 [ME]
- Plows were usually **2-furrow** behind a tractor, taking **2.3 h/acre** (8.2 h/acre with 2 horses). Bottoms were 14 in or larger, plowing 8–10 in deep. Schrumpf41 [ME]; FB1958 [US]
- Plowing capacity per 10-hour day: Farmall C with 2×12 in plows, 6–9 acres; Farmall H with 2×14/16 in, 9–11 acres; Farmall M with 3×14/16 in, 15–17 acres. IH49
- Plowing speed: one 14-in bottom at **2½ mph** covers about 0.3 acre/h. Smith's worked example is 3 bottoms at 3 mph = 10.5 acres/day. Smith64 p.130
- Spring-tooth harrow widths: 10 ft 2 in to 12 ft for 2–3 sections, 15 ft 9 in for the 37-tooth. Tandem disk 6–10 ft at 25–42 acres/day. IH49
- Plowing pattern:
  - Stake a headland "about twice the length of the outfit" all round the field.
  - Plow a marking furrow round the field.
  - Plow the field in lands, opening a second land when the first gets too narrow for a sweeping turn.
  - Plow out the headland last, going round the field.
  - JD52 Fig. 90.
- Dead furrows are about twice the width of one bottom. Turning takes about **6%** of plowing time on a two-plow outfit in a 1,320-ft field. Smith64 pp.93–95
- [EST] Disking and harrowing pattern: lengthwise passes overlapping half a width, then a pass or two across the ends. No Maine source.

## 2. Seed cutting and planting
- Seed was cut by hand, **6 labor-h/acre**, at most about a 2-day supply ahead. FB1958 shows two men at a mechanical cutter in a potato house (Fig. 9), cutting 300+ bu/day. Schrumpf41 [ME]; FB1958
- Early-1970s Mapleton: pairs cut seed at a hand rack. Two experienced cutters did about **one truckload a day**; a machine did 2+. Woodworth [ME]
- Rows were 34 in; seed pieces were spaced under 12 in in Aroostook (8–10 in for Katahdin). That is 18,449 pieces/acre at 34×10 in. FB1958
- Older seed rate: **5–6 barrels/acre** (825–990 lb). USDA FB 1064, 1935. [DERIVED] At 34×10 in with 1.5 oz pieces it is about 17 cwt/acre.
- Fertilizer: "a ton or more to the acre" in Aroostook. FB1958 p.6
- Planter type: a **two-row picker planter** was "still the favorite machine" on farms of 50 acres or less. 3- and 4-row planters were used only on level, stone-free land. FB1958 pp.22–23
- Crew in 1941: "usually a man on the tractor and a second man on the planter", and often "an extra man in the field to help fill the fertilizer and seed hoppers". Planting took **1.5 h/acre** by tractor. Schrumpf41 [ME]
- What the rider does:
  - Early 1970s: the rider sat on the rear seat with a stick to keep seed moving into the picker boxes. Woodworth [ME]
  - On an assisted-feed planter, the rider puts a piece in each empty pocket and takes out doubles. Smith64
- IH No. 5 two-row planter: up to **5 mph**; seed hopper **7½ bu**; fertilizer hopper **350 lb**; rider's platform at the rear. IH49
- John Deere "Two-Twelve": "up to 15 acres a day … at 4 miles per hour"; fertilizer hoppers 350 lb each, seed hoppers 5+ bu each. *American Potato Journal* advertisement, Feb 1956.
- Farmall C with a 2-row planter: **20 acres per 10-hour day** (maker's figure). IH49
- Where it refills:
  - A flatbed truck with seed barrels parks beside the planter; one man tips a barrel into the seed hopper while another empties fertilizer bags. FB1958 1948 ed., Fig. 22 caption, "Aroostook County, Maine" [ME]
  - Early 1970s: the planter pulls alongside a flatbed of fertilizer bags at the **head of the field**. Empty bags go on a tall spike there and are burned later. Then the planter backs up to a conveyor from the seed truck. Woodworth [ME]
- [DERIVED] A full load covers about **0.35–0.5 acre**. On 660-ft rows that is a refill at the same headland, where the trucks stand, about **every 2 round trips**.
- Planting window was mid-May to about 1 June. FB 1064 [US]

## 3. Cultivating and hilling
- In 1941 the cultivator and hiller were on the tractor together (hiller behind the cultivator), done in one pass. **3.7 passes per season**, at 0.9 h/acre each. Schrumpf41 [ME]
- 1964: 2 cultivations plus 1 "spading" (the final weed pass). SoilCons64 [ME]
- Right after planting, the covering discs leave a low ridge with the seed 2–3 in deep. One or two weeder or harrow passes run lengthwise along the rows just before the plants emerge. The ridge is begun at the first cultivation and built up at each later one to **6–8 in high**. Cultivation stops at full bloom or when the vines fill the rows. FB1958 pp.26–27
- Early passes often bury the young plants "once or twice". FB1958. Burying plants by hilling just after emergence cut yield. MAES Bulletin 483, 1950 [ME]
- Speed: **2½–3 mph** (2–2½ mph in heavy weeds). Smith64. A 2-row cultivator covered about 28 acres/day. IH49
- [EST] Final hill about 8–10 in high and 14–18 in wide, flat-topped. Extrapolated from the 6–8 in figure in FB1958.

## 4. Scouting and roguing
- Easton, 1943: roguers walked "down the rows while carefully looking at each plant". "Each had a potato sack slung over her shoulder" and carried a "short-handled three-tined potato digger". They wore broad-brimmed hats. ESR43 [ME]
- Pace in that account: **2 girls rogued 94 acres in 22 days**, about 2.1 acres per person-day [DERIVED]; it is not clear whether that was one pass or several. "Usually boys have done the roguing." ESR43 [ME]
- Pulled plants were "carried to the end of the row for carrying away and destroying". If one hill shows disease, all four hills from the same seed tuber are pulled. Seed growers rogued "many times each summer". ESR42 [ME]
- Roguing took **8 h/acre per season**. Schrumpf41 [ME]
- Timing: start when plants are 4–6 in high, then every week or 10 days through the season. USDA *Yearbook of Agriculture* 1953 pp.148–150. Carry rogues in a closed bag and dispose of them 500 ft or more from the field. USDA Circular 764, 1948.
- State inspectors: at least **2 field inspections about a month apart**, then ring rot inspections. 13 inspectors covered 60,000 acres (1979–82). [ME]
- Modern Oregon guide: rogue 8 a.m. to about noon, sun behind you. [OR, MOD]
- [EST] One roguer per row middle, watching the rows on both sides, at about 1 mph or slower, 3–5 passes from late June to early August.

## 5. Late-blight spraying
- Aroostook 1949–53, conventional rig: **8-row tractor-mounted boom** built by Haines of Presque Isle with a Myers pump, **100–120 gal/acre at 400 psi**. B527 [ME]
- Low-gallonage trailed rigs: 25–30 gpa, tanks 100–200 gal; "most … had 150 gallon capacity". B527 [ME]
- "Travel speed in all cases was **four miles per hour**." Three nozzles per row. B527 [ME] (I confirmed this sentence in the scanned text.)
- Number of sprays: **9–13 per season** (1950–53). Wheels ran in the row middles; no rows were left unplanted for the sprayer. B527 [ME]
- Presque Isle farm, 1954: John Bean **300-gal** mounted sprayer, "nearly a dozen applications". *American Vegetable Grower*, Jul 1955 [ME]
- 1941: sprayed **5 times a season**; **0.7 h/acre per spray including mixing**. Schrumpf41 [ME]
- Wheel damage was smaller with wider booms: crop loss 14.6, 11.0 and 8.7 barrels per acre of field for 6-, 8- and 10-row booms. B460 [ME]
- Boom maker range "8 to 12 row work" (Hardie, 1947). Spray every 7–10 days from plant height of 4–6 in; 60–125 gpa as the plants grow. FB1958
- Northeast 1979: maneb or mancozeb on about **7.5 applications per season**. USDA *Pesticide Use on Fall Potatoes*, 1979 survey [US/NE]
- [DERIVED] The 8-row boom at 34 in is 22.7 ft wide. A 150-gal tank at 100–120 gpa covers **1.25–1.5 acres**, so about 4 tankfuls per 5-acre field (2 with a 300-gal tank).
- Water source: only "hauled a considerable distance to the field" (B527). [EST] Tank truck at the field edge, filled at a brook, pond or the farmyard.

## 6. Vine killing
- Maine adopted vine killing in the late 1930s. Killing "**two weeks or more** prior to harvest" prevents skinning and bruising, and it protects seed from virus and tubers from blight rot. Sprays went on at 140 gpa; dinitros killed the leaves in **2–3 days**. APJ52 [ME]
- Rotobeater: chops everything but 4–5 in of stem. Beaters "cover only **two rows** at a time" and are expensive. Chemicals go through the regular 4–12-row sprayer and kill in 4–10 days. APJ52 [ME]; FB1958 [US]
- Chemicals:
  - Sodium arsenite was used in the 1940s, but MAES advised against it for anything except seed stock. B471, B438 [ME]
  - 1954 Presque Isle farm: Geigy top killer, 2 gal/acre, "two weeks before harvest", put on with the farm's own sprayer. [ME]
  - Northeast 1979: 80% of acreage vine-killed, about 2 weeks before harvest, mostly dinoseb, 1.0–1.4 sprays. [US/NE]
- Aroostook practice in 1982: "killing potato vines and having them ready to harvest in … **14 days**". H.J. Murphy (UMaine), 1982 New Brunswick seminar proceedings [ME]
- [EST] Beater speed 3–4 mph.

## 7. Digging, picking, barrel truck
- Diggers: by the late 1940s "tractors were soon pulling **two-row diggers**", with a man riding the digger to set the cutting depth until hydraulic lifts came in. StarCity [ME]
- How the potatoes lie: the digger leaves the two rows together as **one band on top of the soil**. Farm memoirs (MeInMaine; Carmel Hillbillies 2013) [ME]
- Digger speed: no Maine figure. Idaho Bulletin 283 (1951) recommended **1.5 mph** or less. [US-ID]
- Crew on one 1970 Easton farm:
  - 1 digger driver.
  - 1 barrel-truck driver.
  - 1 man who "rolled barrels on the truck".
  - A foreman.
  - **About 20 pickers aged 9–50**.
  - AFSC70 [ME]
- Another 1970 farm (90 acres): about 38 pickers, 1 barrel-truck driver, and "several" adults on the trucks and in the potato house. AFSC70 [ME]
- "A single one of these machines [a harvester] does the work of a crew of **30 pickers**." AFSC70 [ME]
- Barrels per picker per day, 1970 (named pickers): age 10: 15; age 12: 25; age 13: 35; age 14: 40; age 16: 60; adult women: 50. AFSC70 [ME]
  - Strong pickers recall **100+** a day. *Bangor Daily News* 1 Oct 2012; Yankee 1990 reader comments [ME]
  - Most pickers 20–40. StarCity [ME]
- Pay per barrel: about 20¢ in 1950, 25¢ in 1960 (StarCity); **30¢** in 1970 (AFSC70); 40¢ in 1974–75 (*Bangor Daily News* 2021); 50¢ in 1984 (*Christian Science Monitor* 1984).
- Barrel: **165 lb** by Maine law since 1891 (Johnston72). About **30 in tall × 19½ in across**, cedar staves with ash hoops (MMN #12957). About **4 split-ash baskets** fill one barrel (memoir), so a basket holds about 40 lb [DERIVED].
- Sections: each picker has a section marked with sticks, or a lunch box, jug or coat when sticks run out. Memoirs [ME]
  - Sections seem to have been sized in barrels: on a hot day in 1984 the crew boss gave out "half barrel sections". *Bangor Daily News* 2 Oct 2014 [ME]
- Barrels stand upright "**every other row**", leaving a lane for the truck. Memoirs [ME]
- Tickets: each picker gets a numbered bundle and wedges a ticket into the top stave of each full barrel. The truck man pulls the ticket. At night the household sorted tickets round the table. Memoir; AFSC70 [ME]
- The truck crew changed over time:
  - **1950**: hand loading by **3 men** (2 on the ground, 1 on the truck); **28 barrels** a load, low body with wheel boxes. Vintage Maine Images 27821 [ME]
  - **1946 onward**: battery or electric hoist with a grapple, patented 1949. Crew of **2**, "one loading and one driving", and "the truck could just keep on going". *Bangor Daily News* 20 Jan 2015; MMN #18184, #19231 [ME]
  - Flatbed bodies from the mid-1950s carried **40 barrels**; a memoir says "fifty or more". Barrels went field to house this way "until the 1970s". StarCity; memoir [ME]
- Harvester adoption in Maine: 12 machines in 1955; 150 in 1961; 1,000 in 1967; **1,300 and about 65% of the crop in 1970**; 80–90% in 1971; hand crews "only about 10 percent" in 1984. Johnston72; AFSC70; *Christian Science Monitor* 1984
- Harvester crew: 2–7 sorters riding the machine, often women; in 1970s–80s Aroostook memories, 3–4 pulling rocks. It needs 2–4 bulk trucks alongside, about 120 cwt (≈73 barrels) each, and runs at 4 mph or less. USDA Ag. Handbook 171 (1959); Production Research Report 98 (1967) [US]; memoir [ME]
- [DERIVED] Acres per day: 20 pickers × 40 barrels = 800 barrels. At about 145–175 barrels/acre, that is **5–6 acres a day, about one 5-acre field per day**.
- [EST] Truck loads per day: about 20–30 loads (800–1,200 barrels ÷ 40). Distance-dependent.

## 8. Hauling and the potato house
- The Aroostook house is built on a "sidehill or knoll". It has a ground-level basement driveway at the rear, with bins on either side of the drive. The basement is "8 to 12 feet or more" deep and holds several thousand barrels. Bins are topped off through trap doors in the floor above, and the upper storey stores barrels and tools. FB847 [US, describing the Aroostook type]
- Barrels were unloaded by hand: tipped on the side, rolled "hand-over-hand" on planks laid over the potatoes already in the bin, and dumped. Blackstone, 1950s; Johnston72; c.1940 Caribou photo caption [ME]
- 1950s bins at a Bridgewater, Maine storage held potatoes **4–19 ft deep**. USDA AMS-401, 1960 [ME]
- Bulk era: a 40-ft conveyor filled a 120-cwt truckload into a bin in about **10 minutes**, with 1 man plus the truck driver. USDA AMS-362, 1960 [US, Red River Valley]

## 9. The working day
- Harvest, 1970 Easton farm: wake **5:00**, breakfast **5:15**, in the truck to the fields by **6:00**, back to the house for lunch at **11:00**, in the field again **12:00–17:00**. That is "ten hours plus an hour for lunch". AFSC70 [ME]
- "The crew picked **six days a week excluding Sundays and rainy days**." AFSC70 [ME]
  - Counter-evidence: one farmer dug Sunday mornings to noon (Yankee 1990 reader comment), and a 2011 *Bangor Daily News* piece says "seven days a week". Treat Sunday as off, or a half day when behind.
- Pickers rode to the field "just daybreak" in the back of a truck. On frosty mornings digging waited for the sun to warm the ground. G.J. Smith, *Yankee* Sept 1990 [ME]
- Harvest season ran about 15 Sep–15 Oct. Schools opened in late August and closed for **3 weeks** (sometimes 4) of harvest recess, a custom dating from about 1945. AFSC70; *Yankee* [ME]
  - About **15,000 school children** picked every year from 1950 to 1970, from age 5 up, with up to 8,000 bonded Canadian workers and 2,000 First Nations workers in earlier years. AFSC70 [ME]
- Daylight, Presque Isle (timeanddate.com, current DST clock):

  | Date | Sunrise | Sunset |
  |---|---|---|
  | 15 May | 4:57 | 7:59 pm |
  | 31 May | 4:42 | 8:17 pm |
  | 15 Sep | 6:09 | 6:43 pm |
  | 30 Sep | 6:29 | 6:13 pm |

  Before the Uniform Time Act of 1966, Maine kept standard time by law unless a town adopted daylight time locally (1925 statute), so for 1955–66 subtract 1 hour.
- [EST] Spring and summer hours: about 6:00 to dusk for planting, with a noon dinner at the house. Based on the harvest pattern and the daylight; no planting-hours source was found. Rock picking was done "in the heat of the sun" after school and on Saturdays (Woodworth).

## General: speeds, headlands, walking
- Gear speeds (mph, lowest to highest forward gear; TractorData.com, compiled from manuals; Super C drawbar tested in 2nd gear, Nebraska Test 458):

  | Tractor | Speeds (mph) |
  |---|---|
  | Farmall Super C | 2.4 / 3.8 / 5.0 / 10.3 |
  | Farmall H, M | 2.6 / 3.5 / 4.2 / 5.1 / 16.4 |
  | Ford 8N | 3.7 / 4.8 / 6.5 / 13.6 |
  | John Deere 50 | 1.5 / 2.5 / 3.5 / 4.5 / 5.8 / 10.0 |

- Field work is therefore **2.5–5 mph**: plow about 2.5–3.5; harrow 4–5; plant 3–4 (B527 and the Deere ad both use 4); spray 4; cultivate 2.5–3; dig about 1.5. Road travel is **10–16 mph** in top gear.
- Headlands:
  - Plowing headland "about twice the length of the outfit". JD52
  - Farmall C turning radius **7 ft 11 in**, using brake turns. IH49
  - Mounted cultivator gangs are lifted at the row end. Smith64
  - About 11 s per turn [DERIVED from Smith64's 0.5 min/acre turning time].
- [EST] Row-crop headlands about 20–30 ft, planted last with about 6–10 crosswise rows. A 5.7-ft two-row rig cannot U-turn into the adjacent pass, so it loop-turns or skips passes.
- Walking speed: comfortable adult pace **1.27–1.46 m/s** on a level floor. Bohannon, *Age and Ageing* 26:15–19, 1997, doi:10.1093/ageing/26.1.15
  - Loads of 20–40% of body mass significantly slow the self-selected speed. *Int. J. Environ. Res. Public Health* 19:3927, 2022, PMC8997774
  - A plowed field has a terrain energy factor of about 1.5 against blacktop's 1.0. Review in *J. Human Performance in Extreme Environments* (JHP), Soule–Goldman/Pandolf lineage.
  - [EST] About **1.0 m/s** on tilled ground carrying a 40-lb basket; roguers about 0.4–0.5 m/s.

## What most affects how the work looks from a distance
1. **The harvest field is crowded**: one two-row digger, about 20 pickers (30 or more on bigger crews), each bent over their own staked section, plus a barrel truck with 2–3 men. After about 1965 that becomes 1 harvester with 3–4 people riding it and a bulk truck running alongside.
2. **Filled barrels stand upright on every other row** across the dug ground, each with a ticket in the top stave. The truck drives the lane between them, hoisting barrels (40 a load) without stopping, and drops empties as it goes.
3. **Dug potatoes lie as one double band on top of the soil**. The digger works at a walking pace (about 1.5 mph) and keeps lapping back through the sections. A crew clears about one 5-acre field a day.
4. **The day runs 6:00–17:00** with everyone leaving for lunch at 11:00–12:00. Crews ride in the back of an open truck at daybreak; nobody works on Sundays or in rain, and digging waits on frosty mornings.
5. **The planter refills at the head of the field**, beside a parked flatbed of seed barrels and fertilizer bags, about every 2 round trips. Two people ride it: the tractor driver and a man on the rear seat with a stick. A spike of empty bags stands at the headland.
6. **Spraying is fast and wide**: an 8-row (about 23 ft) boom at 4 mph with a fine mist, refilling from a tank truck after 1.25–1.5 acres, 9–13 times a season about weekly from July.
7. **Most tillage runs straight down the length of the field** after fall plowing: 2–3 passes with a 10–12 ft spring-tooth harrow at 4–5 mph. Spring plowing, in lands with dead furrows and the headland plowed last, is the exception.
8. **The rows change shape through the season**: a low ridge after planting, flattened by a harrow before emergence, then built to a 6–8 in hill over about 3–4 passes at 2.5–3 mph. Cultivation stops once the vines meet across the rows.
9. **Roguers walk slowly down the rows** with a sack over the shoulder and a short three-tined digger. They carry pulled plants to the row end, weekly from late June.
10. **About two weeks before digging, the green field turns brown**: a 2-row beater, or the ordinary sprayer with a killer, in 2–10 days.
